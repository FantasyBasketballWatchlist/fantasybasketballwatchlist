# Borrow the crowd, then out-model the minutes

The best projection systems all do the same thing: they project **playing time separately from per-minute production**, and they tune regression and recency weights **stat by stat against future results** instead of picking them by hand. The best-documented ones (DARKO, EPM, Steamer) still lose, or barely win, where human news matters most. That means minutes, role and availability. Your formula's gap to FantasyPros expert consensus (ECR) is about **0.3 fantasy points per game per player, roughly 3,100 points a season across the top 130**. The research says that gap is mostly **information** (camp news, depth charts, injury timelines, and the averaging of 40+ rankers' independent mistakes), not a weakness in your statistical engine. So the most realistic way to close or beat it is to **add ECR to your formula as a third input**, on a common fantasy-points scale, with near-equal weights. Forecasting research shows that an equal-weight average of different sources usually beats every single source, including the best one. No amount of extra per-stat modelling is likely to beat 40 humans with news on its own. Several ideas you rejected (per-stat padding, recency decay, team-change handling) were probably tested in weaker forms than DARKO and Steamer use. They deserve at most one bundled retest, and even the best published evidence expects their gains to be small. The injury-proneness nulls are probably real. The shortlist below is ranked by expected gain per unit of effort, and each item comes with a test protocol built to survive your multiple-comparisons problem. The deciding test should be the **2026-27 season, with same-date snapshots captured before October 20**.

## Every serious system is playing time × per-minute rates, tuned per stat

The architecture is almost identical across sports and vendors. DARKO weights each past game by β^t, where t is **days** since the game (0 < β < 1, closer to 0 means only recent games count). It fits a separate β for each box-score stat with a numerical optimizer that searches for the values that best predict future results. The fitted half-lives (how long before a game counts half as much) range from **about 1 game for starting role and about 3 games for playing time, through about 17 games for usage, 24 for assist rate and 31 for scoring, to about 1.5 seasons for 3P% and 8 seasons for corner-three percentage** ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)). DARKO runs a Kalman filter (a model that treats each game as a noisy reading of true skill) alongside the decay. It merges the two with a gradient-boosted tree and applies a separate age curve for each stat, corrected for survivor bias. It **raises its learning rate when a player changes teams** and pulls projections back toward average between seasons ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)). EPM, built independently, reached the same design: "different stats have different optimal decay factors based on stabilization rates," with age effects that vary by stat ([Dunks & Threes](https://dunksandthrees.com/about/epm)). LEBRON regresses box stats differently by offensive archetype rather than toward one league mean ([BBall Index](https://www.bball-index.com/lebron-box-prior/)).

The simple baselines show what the extra machinery has to beat. Basketball-Reference's Simple Projection System (SPS) is the baseball system Marcel ported to basketball. It weights seasons 6/3/1, adds **1,000 minutes of league-average production to every stat alike**, and applies one linear age adjustment peaking at 28. It admits it does not really project minutes ([Basketball-Reference](https://www.basketball-reference.com/about/projections.html)). Marcel itself weights seasons 5/4/3, adds 1,200 plate appearances of league average, and projects playing time with a one-line formula: **0.5 × last year + 0.1 × two years ago + 200** ([Tango](http://www.tangotiger.net/archives/stud0346.shtml)). It was built as the "minimum level of competence," and more sophisticated systems deliver "only… incremental improvements" over it ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/the-running-of-the-monkeys/)). Steamer, usually the most accurate baseball system, is essentially Marcel with season weights and **the amount of regression backtested separately for each component** ([MLB glossary](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/projection-systems/steamer)). Comparables-based systems (ZiPS, PECOTA, SCHOENE, CARMELO) have the weakest evidence. In Beyond the Box Score's 2015 grading, Marcel beat both ZiPS and PECOTA in every category ([BtBS 2016](https://www.beyondtheboxscore.com/2016/1/14/10733872/steamer-zips-pecota-marcel-projections-review)). That supports your decision to drop similarity averaging.

The fantasy vendors describe the same skeleton plus a human news layer. Basketball Monster's projections are hand-maintained by named analysts, and full-season numbers "refresh several times a day" ([Basketball Monster](https://basketballmonster.com/Help.aspx)). RotoWire combines historical performance, projected minutes and pace, has analysts review "coaching tendencies, injury recovery timelines, and lineup changes," and says outright that "minutes matter far more than a single game's shooting percentage" ([RotoWire](https://www.rotowire.com/basketball/projections.php)). Hashtag Basketball sells BLEND7/BLEND14 sets, which are 50/50 mixes of its projection with the last 7 or 14 days ([Hashtag](https://hashtagbasketball.com/fantasy-basketball-projections)). That is a quiet admission that recent box scores reveal role changes before the base model catches up. ESPN publishes no methodology at all. Its points-league rankings come from named analysts, mainly Andre Snellings ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/fantasy/basketball/story/_/id/42898411/fantasy-basketball-rankings-projections-points-h2h)). None of these vendors publishes an accuracy record.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DARKO | Separate, recent role; its weakest stat | Per stat, per possession | Fitted β per stat, in days | Fitted padding per stat | Per stat, survivor-corrected | Self-reported win vs DFS sites except minutes |

## Your gap to the experts is mostly news, availability and averaging

The only direct evidence on consensus accuracy is from football, but it points one way. In FantasyPros' 2010 NFL contest, the all-expert consensus finished **#2 of 40+ experts**. A top-5 consensus scored 61.6%, above the best individual (61.3%), and consensus beat every individual over the final four weeks ([FantasyPros](https://www.fantasypros.com/2011/01/expert-consensus-rankings-accuracy/)). ECR adds up "Rank Points" across more than 40 NBA outlets rather than averaging raw ranks ([FantasyPros support](https://support.fantasypros.com/hc/en-us/articles/115001219327-What-is-ECR-Expert-Consensus-Rankings-and-how-do-you-calculate-it); [FantasyPros NBA experts](https://www.fantasypros.com/experts/nba/)). FantasyPros publishes **no NBA accuracy leaderboard**, so nobody knows how much the best basketball experts beat NBA ECR. The general forecasting literature explains why a consensus is hard to beat. Across 30 comparisons, equal-weight combinations cut errors by **about 12.5% on average (3–24%)**, and the gains are largest when the sources use different information ([Armstrong](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2001/01/96-JSA-Combining-Forecasts.pdf)). In baseball's 2014 evaluation, the average of projection systems (wOBA MAE 0.0270) beat every single system (0.0274–0.0289) ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/evaluating-the-2014-projection-systems/)). ECR is itself such an average, while your blend combines only two sources.

The information advantage shows up wherever human sources touch playing time. DARKO calls minutes "the hardest thing any projection system forecasts," and minutes was **the only stat where it lost** to human-informed DFS projections ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)). (The same page elsewhere lists minutes among the stats where it outperformed, so treat this as indicative.) Availability behaves the same way. Your preseason lost-season AUC of 0.61–0.65 from games-played history matches the public evidence: the impressive 0.80–0.87 AUCs in the injury literature predict the **next game** from features like current missed-game streaks and minutes changes, and age ranks among the least useful features ([Wang, Wharton](https://wsb.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/WANG_Using-Injury-Risk-Forecasting-to-Quantify-Financial-Impact-in-the-NBA-1.pdf); [McClung, Rochester](https://hajim.rochester.edu/ece/sites/zduan/teaching/ece408/projects/2025/MaxMcClung_NBAInjuryRiskAndAvailabilityAnalysis_FinalReport.pdf)). What is left to predict is news: offseason surgery, recovery timelines, camp reports. That is why ESPN's projected games helped you and 23 box-score proneness signals did not. Even the 65-game rule barely moved star availability, which is "dominated by injury spells the rule does not touch" ([L-Maddock analysis](https://github.com/L-Maddock/nba-65-game-rule)).

Two things temper this story. First, the one peer-reviewed NBA study found that paid daily projections beat naive past-performance forecasts "only to a moderate extent," were "inefficient and, in some cases, even biased," and differed only slightly from one another in the long run ([Döpke, Köhler & Tegtmeier 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jecfin/v48y2024i1d10.1007_s12197-023-09646-7.html)). Human sources are not magic. Their errors are correctable, which is exactly why combining them with a statistical model should help. Second, ECR is a **roto/category list** and still beat a points-league blend. Being optimized for the wrong scoring format should have cost it value, so whatever it knows must be worth more than the format mismatch. That again points to information (who plays, how much, and how often) rather than per-stat scoring skill. One untested possibility you should rule out is **timing**. If the archived ECR snapshots were taken closer to opening night than your ESPN projection pull, part of ECR's edge is simply later news ([FantasyPros accuracy method](https://www.fantasypros.com/accuracy/)). Keep in mind too that 4 wins out of 4 seasons is suggestive but cannot reach conventional significance on a season-level sign test (p ≈ 0.125 two-sided). Paired per-player errors are the right way to size the gap.

## Which of your null results were too weak, and which are probably real

Several of your rejections match the weak versions of ideas whose strong versions carry the published systems. DARKO and Steamer never hand-pick one decay or one padding amount for all stats. They **fit a separate constant for each stat by optimizer, against a forward-looking target**. Medvedovsky's padding study searched, stat by stat, for the number of league-average observations to add to year-to-date totals that best predicted rest-of-season performance. He fit it on about 750,000 player-games and landed on **about 240 attempts for 3P%**. He also found minutes "extremely stable… almost entirely a coach's decision" ([kmedved.com](https://kmedved.com/2020/08/06/nba-stabilization-rates-and-the-padding-approach/)). SPS's single 1,000-minute pad over-regresses stable stats like rebounding and assist rate and under-regresses noisy ones like 3P%, steals and blocks. Your "SPS averaging" test therefore compared against a deliberately untuned baseline ([Basketball-Reference](https://www.basketball-reference.com/about/projections.html)). Decay applied to per-game fantasy totals mixes a component that should decay in games (minutes, role) with components that should decay over seasons (shooting). Any single window is wrong for most of the parts.

The other side deserves equal weight, and it is why these ideas rank low rather than high below. Your target is ESPN points per game, and it is dominated by volume (points, rebounds, assists, shot attempts) multiplied by minutes. Rebounding and assist rates are extremely stable year to year. Paine found correlations of **0.974 for assist rate and 0.944 for offensive rebound rate** among players staying on the same team ([Basketball-Reference](https://www.basketball-reference.com/blog/index0597.html?p=8472)). When the parts that dominate the total are this stable, fancier regression of the noisy parts (3P%, FT%, steals, blocks) moves the total very little. Marcel's history says the same: sophistication buys "incremental" gains at best ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/the-running-of-the-monkeys/)). No one has published an NBA backtest showing that projecting components beats projecting fantasy points directly. So your nulls on per-stat recency, age and padding are **plausibly real for veterans**. A stronger retest might find a small rate-side gain, most likely among young bigs and fluky shooters, and it should be run once, bundled, not as three separate fishing trips.

| Rejected idea | What the strong version looks like | Likely verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Per-stat padding | Separate pad per stat fitted on held-out seasons, toward a position or role prior, on per-minute rates | Earlier test probably too weak; gain likely small |
| Per-stat recency / decay windows / older seasons | Days-based β fitted per stat, fast for minutes and slow for shooting, on rates not totals | Probably too weak for preseason; small upside |
| Per-stat age | Survivor-corrected delta method on regressed rates, bigs vs guards | Real for most players; possible edge only at age extremes |
| Changed-team flag | Not a mean shift but **more weight on outside sources** for team changers | Earlier test asked the wrong question; worth one retest |
| Usage-weighted teammate boost / proportional minutes | Per-stat displacement plus a 240-minute team budget normalized by talent rank | Evidence is 2010-era and heuristic; low priority |
| SPS similarity, Cox hazards, 23 proneness signals, coaching change | Nothing stronger in the literature | Nulls probably real; stop |

## A ranked shortlist of seven tests

| Rank | Build | Overlaps a rejected idea? | Honest expected gain | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Three-source blend: BPV + ESPN + ECR converted to points per game | No (your own "next idea") | Largest: plausibly closes most of the ~0.3 FPTS/G gap, may edge ECR | Low |
| 2 | Gap decomposition: minutes vs rate vs games vs slice | No (diagnostic) | None directly; decides items 3–6 | Low |
| 3 | Blend outside sources on minutes only; keep your own per-minute rate | Partly (37.5% ESPN blend) | Small to moderate, uncertain | Low–medium |
| 4 | Slice weights: more outside weight for team changers, second-year players, injury-news players | Yes (changed-team flag) | Small; concentrated where your blend is weakest | Low |
| 5 | Stat-specific fitted padding and days-based decay on per-minute rates (one bundled test) | Yes (per-stat padding/recency, decay windows, SPS) | Small; likely under 0.05 FPTS/G overall | Medium–high |
| 6 | Rookie prior from draft slot × age, weighted heavily toward outside sources | Partly ("rookie draft age") | Moderate for rookies only; matters for drafts, not the veteran gap | Medium |
| 7 | Per-stat aging for young and old bigs only | Yes (per-stat age) | Very small | Medium |

### 1. Put ECR on your scale and average it in

Build a curve that turns an ECR rank into expected points per game, and a second curve that turns it into expected season total, **fitted only on seasons other than the one being tested**. Fit realized value against ECR rank each season, smooth it with isotonic or exponential fitting, and average the curves. FantasyPros scores its own experts the same way, turning each rank slot into the points that slot historically produced ([FantasyPros](https://www.fantasypros.com/accuracy/)). Averaging raw ranks would treat the gap between 1st and 10th like the gap between 200th and 210th ([DynastyProcess](https://dynastyprocess.com/values/)). Then combine BPV, ESPN and converted ECR. The combination-puzzle literature says estimated weights rarely beat equal weights over short histories, because the error in estimating the weights eats the theoretical gain. After a multiple-comparisons correction, the European Central Bank's forecaster panel found "only a very modest case" for anything but equal weights ([Genre et al.](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/timmermann/SPF_IJoF_2012_06_22.pdf); [Wang, Hyndman et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.04216)). The one exception in that paper is instructive. Flexible weights helped when the equal-weight average had a **persistent bias**, and a stats model that ignores news may carry exactly that kind of bias.

**Test.** Pre-register exactly three candidates: current blend; equal thirds (BPV/ESPN/ECR); and ECR-heavy 0.25/0.25/0.5. Fit the rank-to-points curve leave-one-season-out (LOSO: fit on three seasons, score the fourth, rotate). Score on the fixed pool described below, on both targets (per-game and season total). Report paired per-player error differences with bootstrap intervals clustered by player. Run draft-simulation Design B one variant at a time. The one season of FantasyPros ESPN-points ECR is a sanity check only, never a tuning set. **Expected gain:** since ECR alone already beats your blend by ~3,100 points a season, a blend that roughly matches ECR is the floor you should expect. Beating ECR by a further few percent is what the combination literature predicts, but it is not guaranteed with correlated sources and four seasons.

### 2. Decompose the gap before building anything else

For each player in the pool, split the error of your blend and of ECR's implied projection into three parts: minutes per game (projected vs actual), points per minute (with minutes fixed at actual), and games played. Then cut the paired differences by slice: team changers, players with preseason injury news or surgery, second-year players, ages ≤23 and ≥32, and the top 50. This is the test FantasyPros' own methodology implies, and it follows directly from the pattern that human sources win on minutes and availability ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about); [RotoWire](https://www.rotowire.com/basketball/projections.php)). **Expected gain:** none on its own. But if more than half the gap sits in minutes and games, items 3 and 4 move up and item 5 can be dropped. If the rate component carries the gap, item 5 moves up. It costs a day and makes every later test better aimed.

### 3. Take minutes from the humans, keep your own rate

Your current 37.5% ESPN blend mixes ESPN's whole per-game number into yours. The FanGraphs Depth Charts design separates the two jobs: it averages the statistical systems' rates and **overlays a separate human playing-time projection** ([FanGraphs](https://library.fangraphs.com/principles/projections/)). ZiPS does not project playing time at all, by design ([FanGraphs](https://blogs.fangraphs.com/the-2026-zips-projections-are-almost-here/)). If ESPN's API projection includes minutes, compare three forecasts: your rate × ESPN minutes, your rate × a 50/50 blend of your minutes and ESPN's, and the current blend. If it does not include minutes, this item depends on finding another minutes source and should wait. **Expected gain:** small to moderate. It could lose, because your 37.5% blend may already capture most of this. **Test:** same LOSO, same pool, three pre-registered variants, and report the minutes-error component separately so you can see whether the mechanism is the one claimed.

### 4. Give outside sources more weight where news matters

This is a weight change, not a mean shift, and that is the difference from your rejected changed-team flag. DARKO does not add or subtract value for a new team. It **trusts its history less** ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)). Paine's data shows why: true shooting correlation falls from **0.706 to 0.556** for team changers, while assist and rebound rates barely move ([Basketball-Reference](https://www.basketball-reference.com/blog/index0597.html?p=8472)). Pelton reports that adding consensus rank and draft slot improves rookie projections ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/16235135/explaining-kevin-pelton-nba-draft-projection-system)). Allow at most three slices (team changer, second-year player, preseason injury news) with one extra outside-source weight each, chosen from {base, base + 0.15, base + 0.30}, LOSO. **Expected gain:** small overall but concentrated in exactly the players where your blend is most exposed. It is only worth keeping if the slice gains survive LOSO and the rest of the pool is not harmed.

### 5. One bundled, properly fitted retest of per-stat regression and decay

Project per-minute rates for each scoring component (PTS, 3PM, FGA/FGM, FTA/FTM, REB, AST, STL, BLK, TOV) and multiply by projected minutes. Give each stat its own padding constant, toward a position or role mean rather than the league mean, and its own days-based decay. Fit both with `scipy.optimize.differential_evolution` against the next season's per-minute rates, inside each LOSO fold. Keep minutes on a separate, fast decay. This is the Steamer and DARKO recipe ([MLB glossary](https://www.mlb.com/glossary/projection-systems/steamer); [kmedved.com](https://kmedved.com/2020/08/06/nba-stabilization-rates-and-the-padding-approach/)). You can fit on your older, unused seasons back to about 2001 **only for the per-stat constants**, which keeps them away from your backtest seasons. The 2014-15 to 2018-19 holdout seasons are already spent. **Expected gain:** small, likely under 0.05 FPTS/G overall, because the volume stats that dominate your scoring are already stable. The upside is concentrated in young bigs and fluky shooting seasons. **Test:** compare per-minute accuracy **with minutes held at actual**, so the rate side is isolated, then the full projection. Treat it as one comparison, not a grid, and drop it if it does not win on paired errors.

### 6. A real rookie prior, and let the experts lead on rookies

DARKO starts rookies from **age, draft slot and height**, then learns fast ([DARKO About](https://www.darko.app/about)). Pelton finds summer league correlates only about 0.20 with the regular season, compared with about 0.30 for pre-draft projections ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/espn/print?id=20122364)). Fit per-game points on draft slot × age from your draft history. The payoff is in drafts: your backtest lists had no rookie projections while the analysts' did, and ADP's whole edge over your blend came from rookies. **Expected gain:** moderate for the rookie slice, zero for the veteran gap. **Test:** rookie-only paired errors LOSO, then Design B draft simulation with rookies included in the pool.

### 7. Per-stat aging only at the extremes

Blocks and offensive rebounds peak at about 20–23, while assists and 3-point volume keep rising into the early 30s ([Brahme & Franks](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.33752)). An age bucket on total fantasy points averages these trends out, which matters only for young and old bigs. **Expected gain:** very small. Test it inside item 5's bundle rather than as a separate comparison.

## Test so that a real gain can be told from luck

With about 105 ideas already tried on the same few seasons, the biggest danger is adopting a lucky winner. Apply one protocol to every item. **Fixed pool:** the union of each source's preseason top 150 and the actual top 150. Keep injured players, with missed games counted in season totals, because dropping them favors whichever source ignored injury risk ([FantasyPros NFL method](https://www.fantasypros.com/about/faq/football-inseason-accuracy-methodology/)). **Same-date snapshots:** ESPN, ECR and your inputs frozen as of the same day, so no source wins on later news. **Pre-registration:** at most three variants per item, written down before running. **Leave-one-season-out** for every fitted number. **Paired per-player errors:** report per-player differences on both per-game and season-total targets, with bootstrap intervals clustered by player and Spearman rank correlation as a secondary metric ([THT](https://tht.fangraphs.com/evaluating-the-2014-projection-systems/)). **Adoption:** adopt a variant only if it wins at least 3 of 4 seasons, its paired interval excludes zero, and it does no harm in the top 50. Because Genre et al.'s apparent winners mostly failed a multiple-comparisons check, apply a reality-check style correction whenever more than a handful of variants are compared ([Genre et al.](https://rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/timmermann/SPF_IJoF_2012_06_22.pdf)). Finally, the decisive test is prospective. Capture ESPN projections, FantasyPros ECR (roto and ESPN-points), ADP and your formula's output **on the same day before the 2026-27 opener**, freeze the chosen blend, and score it once the season ends.

## Conclusion

No public statistical model beats well-informed humans at predicting playing time and availability. The things your formula can model better than them (regressed per-minute rates, per-stat aging, schedule and injury-report handling in season) are the parts that matter least for a preseason points ranking. The winning move is therefore not to out-model ECR but to **absorb it**: treat expert consensus as a measurement of news you cannot see, put it on your fantasy-points scale, and let your statistics correct its known biases. The combination literature says that pairing should beat either alone. The most useful single result you could get is a clean decomposition of where the 0.3-point gap lives. If it lives in minutes and games, the rate-side ideas you rejected were rightly rejected. If it lives in rates, the fitted per-stat retest is worth running once, bundled, and then closing for good.
