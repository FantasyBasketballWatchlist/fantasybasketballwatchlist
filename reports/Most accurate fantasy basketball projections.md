# Nobody has measured the most accurate source

No one publicly measures which fantasy basketball projection source is most accurate. There is no FantasyPros NBA accuracy leaderboard, no independent multi-season backtest of ESPN, Yahoo, CBS, RotoWire, Basketball Monster, Hashtag Basketball or any other season-long source, and none of those sites publishes an accuracy number for its own NBA projections. The best evidence for ESPN-style head-to-head (H2H) points leagues is **FantasyPros' expert consensus rankings (ECR)**, meaning an average of many analysts' rankings. That evidence comes from two places: a 2010 football study where the consensus finished 2nd of 40+ experts, and **your own backtest**, which is probably the only published-or-not season-long NBA comparison of these sources that exists. Even your backtest gives a split verdict. The consensus picked the most valuable top 130 better than your formula in all 4 seasons and had the best or near-best pick list every season, but on rank correlation across the top 150 it never finished first. Your blend won that metric twice and average draft position (ADP) won it twice. Everything else is weaker: DARKO's wins are self-reported and cover daily stats, public models such as DARKO, EPM, LEBRON and Kevin Pelton's are tested only on team win totals, and the one academic study covers four daily-fantasy providers over one month. The practical result is that "most accurate" is an open question you can answer better than anyone publishing today, for about $0 to $75 a season, if you snapshot the right sources the day before each opener and score them the same way. For your formula (BPV), the goal is not to beat one named expert. It is to beat an average of experts, and the evidence says the way to do that is to include that average in your blend.

### FantasyPros scores experts in football and baseball, not basketball

The question most people start with is "who won FantasyPros' NBA accuracy contest?" There isn't one. The NBA accuracy URL returns a 404 error. Searches of the Wayback Machine at several likely addresses found no captures from 2013 to 2025, even though the same method finds FantasyPros' NFL and MLB accuracy pages ([Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/web/2019/https://www.fantasypros.com/nfl/accuracy/)). FantasyPros' accuracy FAQ describes only football, where it scores the absolute gap between an expert's projected points and actual points ([FantasyPros](https://www.fantasypros.com/accuracy/)). Its baseball contest turns rankings into values and locks the player pool to an ECR cutoff ([FantasyPros, Feb 2026](https://www.fantasypros.com/2026/02/most-accurate-fantasy-baseball-rankings-experts-2025-results/)). The NBA experts page says in boilerplate that it has "accuracy ratings," but the page shows no scores ([FantasyPros NBA experts](https://www.fantasypros.com/experts/nba/)). This conclusion rests on converging absence of evidence rather than a FantasyPros statement, but it is strong enough that you should treat any "most accurate NBA expert per FantasyPros" claim as unverified. One consequence is that **nobody knows whether any individual basketball expert beats the NBA consensus**, which is the question football answers every year.

### Site accuracy claims are absent, not just unverified

The named sites do not claim accuracy at all. Basketball Monster's FAQ and membership pages, Hashtag's projections page and RotoWire's methodology article make no accuracy claims ([BBM](https://basketballmonster.com/Help.aspx); [Hashtag](https://hashtagbasketball.com/fantasy-basketball-projections); [RotoWire](https://www.rotowire.com/basketball/article/fantasy-basketball-rankings-vs-projections-96956)). CBS carries a disclaimer that it "makes no representation or warranty as to the accuracy" ([CBS](https://www.cbssports.com/fantasy/basketball/projections/)). ESPN's projection articles by André Snellings contain no methodology and no track record ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/fantasy/basketball/story/_/id/42898411/fantasy-basketball-rankings-projections-points-h2h)). The one ESPN "most accurate" award that turns up in searches, Mike Clay's, is for NFL ([ESPN Press Room](https://espnpressroom.com/bio/mike-clay/)). No searches turned up accuracy claims for NumberFire/FanDuel Research, Yahoo, Razzball, RotoBaller, SportsLine or Fantrax NBA projections. Reddit could not be searched, so community backtests on r/fantasybball are the one pool of evidence this research could not check. Search there yourself before assuming none exist.

## Consensus earns the top spot by analogy and by your backtest

### Football supplies the only direct consensus-versus-expert numbers

The only FantasyPros study that places ECR against individual experts covers the 2010 NFL season. The consensus of all 40+ experts scored 60.5% and **ranked 2nd overall**, finishing in the top 10 at every position ([FantasyPros, 2011](https://www.fantasypros.com/2011/01/expert-consensus-rankings-accuracy/)). A "top 5 consensus" scored higher, at 61.6%, but it picked the five experts using that same season's results, so it had hindsight and is not a fair benchmark. The broader football evidence points the same way. Fantasy Football Analytics' 11-season NFL study found that a simple average of sources beat individual sources in **63% of head-to-head comparisons**, and that weighting sources by past accuracy did not help because "source accuracy simply does not persist reliably enough from season to season" ([FFA, Sep 2026](https://fantasyfootballanalytics.net/2026/09/we-analyzed-11-seasons-of-dfs-projections-heres-what-we-found.html)). Basketball's version of this pattern appears in the APBRmetrics team-win contests, where the simple average of entries finishes in the top 2 to 5 most years while the winner changes almost every year ([APBR 2022-23](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?t=9946&start=150)). The logic carries over to fantasy basketball even though no one has measured it directly: when sources are close in skill and you cannot tell in advance which one will be best, the average is the safest bet.

### Your backtest is the NBA evidence, and it is split by metric

Your comparison used pre-opener Wayback snapshots of each list, scored on actual ESPN points ([your sources file](../data/rankings/SOURCES.md); [your results](../analysis/test_public_rankings.json)). Two metrics tell different stories. The first is the **top-130 sum**: add up the actual season fantasy points of the 130 players each list ranked highest. It rewards getting the right players into your draft pool. The second is **Spearman rank correlation**, which measures how well a list ordered the top 150 relative to what actually happened. A value of 1 means perfect order.

| Season | Best top-130 sum | FP roto ECR | Your BPV+ESPN blend | Best Spearman (top 150) | ECR Spearman |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2021-22 | ECR 264,605 | 264,605 | 254,861 | Blend 0.578 | 0.548 |
| 2023-24 | ESPN analysts 304,358 | 303,853 | 296,420 | FP ADP average 0.692 | 0.686 |
| 2024-25 | ECR 283,965 | 283,965 | 281,849 | Blend 0.581 | 0.555 |
| 2025-26 | FP ESPN-points ECR 263,478 | 262,261 | 258,361 | Yahoo ADP 0.568 | 0.494 |

Four conclusions follow. First, **FantasyPros consensus is the best-supported source for building a draft list for ESPN points**. The roto ECR had the top pick list in 2 of 4 seasons and was within about 1,300 points of the best in the other 2, even though it ranks for roto categories rather than points. The ESPN-points version had the best list in the only season you could test. Second, **ESPN's analysts' points rankings are a close second**. They won 2023-24 outright and were within 1,000 points of the top in 2025-26. Their bad 2021-22 result came from an archived list last updated in August, two months before the opener. Third, **"ECR is most accurate" depends on the metric**. On rank correlation, ECR never finished first, your blend won twice, and ADP won twice. The consensus is best at identifying who belongs in the top 130, not at ordering all 150. Fourth, the timing explanation does not fully account for ECR's edge. Its 2023-24 snapshot is dated September 25, a month before the opener and earlier than ESPN's, yet it still beat your blend by about 7,400 points. (Most of that gap is rookies: the backtest version of your blend ranks every rookie last, and 2023-24 was Wembanyama's rookie year. With rookies removed from every list the gap is about 1,900 points, and the equal-thirds blend of your formula, ESPN and the consensus tied the consensus that season.) Its biggest win, 2021-22, did come from a snapshot one day before the opener.

Treat these results as suggestive, not proven. A 4-for-4 record gives a two-sided sign test p of about 0.125. The gaps between the top lists in a season (often under 1,000 points on about 280,000) are small enough that one injury can flip the order. And when you converted ECR ranks into point values, it had **worse season-total error than your formula** (+22 points per player, 0 of 4 seasons), because ranks produce coarse values ([your methodology, Sec. 16](../docs/BPV-methodology.md)). In short, the consensus picks the right players, and your formula assigns better numbers to them.

## Public models and daily projections answer different questions

### Team-win contests rank the analytics models, not their fantasy lines

DARKO, EPM, LEBRON, Kevin Pelton and FiveThirtyEight are mostly evaluated through APBRmetrics' preseason team-win contests, where a player metric is combined with someone's minutes guesses to predict each team's win total. No model wins consistently. In 2022-23, the leaders on average error were dtkavana (5.07), the average of all entries (5.45) and Pelton (5.60), with LEBRON at 5.72 and DARKO at 5.81 ([APBR 2022-23](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?t=9946&start=150)). In 2024-25, dtkavana won again at 7.50 and Vegas scored 8.40. DARKO was not entered, and its creator later reported 7.24, which would have won, but that score is self-reported after the season ([APBR 2024-25](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?t=10146&start=240)). In 2021-22, FiveThirtyEight finished 4th and Vegas 6th ([APBR 2021-22](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?t=9903&start=90)). In 2020-21, ESPN's expert aggregate beat Vegas ([APBR 2020-21](https://apbr.org/metrics/viewtopic.php?p=37970)). Entrants also use different minutes sources; DARKO's 2024-25 entry reportedly used Basketball Monster minutes. So these contests test the whole pipeline, not the player model.

EPM's best-known win is its creator's test of predicting next-season team net rating, where it had the lowest error among ten metrics from 2013-14 to 2019-20 ([Dunks & Threes](https://dunksandthrees.com/blog/metric-comparison)). That test is not independent, and plus/minus impact is not fantasy production. FiveThirtyEight's RAPTOR projections stopped updating after 2023 ([538](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/how-our-raptor-metric-works/)). Pelton's per-game stats reach you only inside ESPN's fantasy projections, and no accuracy test of them was found ([ESPN](https://www.espn.com/nba/story/_/id/9797404/explaining-schoene-projection-system)). Basketball-Reference's Simple Projection System (SPS) has no published accuracy test. It is still useful as a naive baseline, because it is the kind of simple forecast any paid source should beat ([Basketball-Reference](https://www.basketball-reference.com/about/projections.html)).

### DARKO is the one model with a box-score claim, and it grades itself

DARKO is the only public model that claims to beat fantasy-style projections on individual stats. Its site says it "beat both sites in every stat tested" against two paid DFS projection services, "some by substantial margins." It also says minutes "remain difficult to forecast," and your earlier research found the same page elsewhere describing minutes as the one stat where it lost ([darko.app](https://www.darko.app/about)). The sites, seasons, sample size and error metric are not published, the test was run by DARKO's creator, and it concerns next-game projections rather than preseason season averages. Still, DARKO is free, publishes per-stat projections with CSV export, and is built by someone who has actually tested against human projections, so it is the strongest free candidate to add to your leaderboard.

### The only academic study is one month of daily projections

Döpke, Köhler and Tegtmeier are independent economists. They evaluated **1,658 projections from four DFS providers in February 2022**: Daily Fantasy Nerd, RotoGrinders, Daily Fantasy Fuel and FantasyPros. They found that professional forecasts beat a naive past-performance forecast "only to a moderate extent" (under 10%), were "inefficient and, in some cases, even biased," and showed only "small, although in one case significant, long-run differences" ([Döpke et al. 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/jecfin/v48y2024i1d10.1007_s12197-023-09646-7.html)). According to snippets of the paywalled text, every provider's mean absolute error (MAE, the average size of a miss) was about 8 DraftKings points against an outcome spread of about 14. Daily Fantasy Nerd and the free Daily Fantasy Fuel were significantly better than the others, and the most expensive provider ($64.99 a month) did not win ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12197-023-09646-7)). Three lessons carry over to season-long play. Price does not predict accuracy. "Inefficient" means a simple recalibration, such as stretching or shrinking a source's projections, can improve it. And the edge over a trailing average is small and comes mostly from minutes and news. None of the premium simulation shops (SaberSim, Stokastic, ETR, FantasyLabs) publishes an audited NBA accuracy figure; their "most accurate" claims are marketing ([SaberSim](https://www.sabersim.com/nba/projections); [Stokastic](https://www.stokastic.com/nba)).

### How to score so the leaderboard means something

Apply your holdout rules to every source equally. **Capture everything on the same day** so that no source wins just by having later news. Build the player pool from the union of the sources' preseason top 150s, not from actual results, because your round-10 work showed that a pool chosen by outcomes can flip rankings. Keep injured players in the pool, with their missed games counting in season totals, since dropping them rewards sources that ignored injury risk. Score **four things**: the top-130 sum (a proxy for draft value), Spearman correlation over the pool, season-total error per player, and per-game error per player, with the last two only for sources that publish stat lines. Report paired per-player differences against the consensus with bootstrap intervals clustered by player. Also report each source's calibration slope, since Döpke et al. found professional projections biased in ways a slope correction can fix. Include the naive baseline and the equal-weight average as permanent entries. Finally, do not crown a winner from one season. In the APBRmetrics contests, neighboring ranks differ by fractions of a win and the winner changes almost every year, and in football, source skill does not persist. Judge on at least three seasons, and expect the equal-weight average to be the entry that is hardest to beat.

## What this means for BPV

The evidence changes what "beating the best" means. There is no single best named source to target. The best-supported benchmark is a consensus, and a consensus is hard to beat for a structural reason: averaging many analysts cancels their individual mistakes about minutes, roles and injuries, which are the things statistical models predict worst. Your backtest suggests the two approaches have complementary strengths. The consensus is better at deciding **who** belongs in the top 130. Your formula is better at **how many points** each player will score, and at ordering the full top 150 in 2 of 4 seasons. The equal-thirds blend of BPV, ESPN and consensus tying the consensus on pick lists while keeping your better point values is the result the combination literature predicts. It is also the most plausible way to beat the best: not a better standalone formula, but a blend in which your formula corrects the consensus's coarse values and the consensus supplies information your formula cannot see.

## Conclusion

The honest answer to "which source is most accurate" is that the fantasy basketball industry has never measured it. Your four-season backtest, flawed as any four-season test is, is probably the most direct evidence that exists, and it supports a narrower claim than the one usually made: expert consensus is the best-evidenced way to **choose** your top 130 for ESPN points, while no source has shown it assigns better **point values** than a good statistical formula. That makes the 2026-27 prospective test more important than any further backtesting. It is the first comparison where no source can benefit from hindsight, and if you run it every year against a fixed field of free and cheap sources, within three seasons you will have the only multi-season NBA fantasy accuracy leaderboard around. The most likely result, based on football, forecasting research and your own data, is that the leaderboard's top spot will belong to an average rather than to any single site. If that happens, your formula's job is to be the ingredient that makes the average better.
